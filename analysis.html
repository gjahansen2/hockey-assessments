What do your assessment scores support?

Upload anonymized integer ratings to explore rankings, close decisions, and uncertainty about underlying skill. This tool analyzes one set of observed scores. Model draws are plausible explanations of those scores, not additional evaluations.

Your scores stay in this browser. There is no server upload or storage. Use invented player and evaluator IDs; remove names and other identifying information before pasting or uploading. Refreshing the page clears the analysis.

1. Choose an analysis and enter scores

Each row is one evaluator’s rating of one player in one session. Use the same evaluator ID whenever that person returns. Use a different ID for a different person. Set goalie to Y for goalies and N for skaters. One file can contain both groups; the selector analyzes them separately. Session IDs are ordered by their first appearance in the file.

player,goalie,session,evaluator,score — for example, P01,N,D1,E01,5. Scores must be integers from 1 to 7. Omit unobserved ratings; never substitute zero. Do not include totals or averages.

3. Incorporate uncertainty

Model assumptions — adjust and rerun to check sensitivity

A higher standard deviation (SD) allows more variation. These values are assumptions, not quantities established by this upload. Explore reasonable alternatives before interpreting a close decision.

Persistent evaluator scoring offsets and scale widths are inferred from the scores. The ordered-probit model treats 1–7 as seven categories, with no rounding. It includes performance shared by evaluators within a session and a persistent evaluator–player effect. Skill has a Normal(4, 1.7) prior bounded to 1–7. Evaluator offsets have a Normal(0, 1) prior; widths have a lognormal prior (untruncated mean 1, SD 0.5), bounded to 0.5–1.75. Category thresholds are fixed at −2.5, −1.5, −0.5, 0.5, 1.5 and 2.5. Numerical skill differences depend on these anchors.

The goalie defaults follow the report: performance SD 0.5 then 1, rating error SD 0.5, and persistent evaluator–player SD 0. The input settings allow alternative assumptions, including persistent evaluator preferences. Higher uncertainty on later sessions reflects less representative performances; fewer ratings separately reduce the information available.

What this tool does—and what it cannot establish. Skater probabilities refer to rank boundaries across the uploaded player pool. They do not reproduce staged eligibility cuts, subjective score breaks, positional needs, or exact final team assignments. Include the complete relevant pool. Missing ratings contribute no evidence. Comparing different scrimmage groups assumes their rating scales are comparable; competition effects and selective recording can invalidate that assumption. Results describe evidence about skill, not emotional readiness or overall team fit.

The margin probabilities concern the absolute underlying skill difference: either player could be better. A high probability of being within a margin supports closeness under the model. A low probability that Y is better does not, by itself, show they are close.

Technical details and responsible interpretation

Four independent seeded MCMC chains use truncated-normal latent-category sampling, Gibbs updates for skill and random effects, and Metropolis updates for evaluator widths. The first third of iterations are discarded; every fourth remaining draw is kept. Split-chain R̂ and a pooled autocorrelation effective sample size (ESS) assess mixing for skills, ranks, and pairwise differences. A warning appears if R̂ exceeds 1.01 or ESS falls below 400. Passing these checks does not prove the model is correct. The implementation is an exploratory adaptation of the report’s skater model; the goalie fit now uses the same full ordinal model rather than the report’s earlier approximation.

Shared scoring offsets can cancel in paired comparisons, but different scale widths, persistent impressions, and performances still matter. Repeated ratings from the same evaluator are not independent panels. The analysis assumes the stated noise levels and scoring mechanism, and does not estimate test–retest team-change probabilities. Do not treat a tiny score difference or these estimates alone as a placement directive. Use the results to guide consistent further review and communicate limitations.
